# Editorial review rubric

Use this rubric to identify concrete problems in a draft, not to certify authorship or make the prose “look human.” It is an editorial heuristic; [sources.md](sources.md) distinguishes it from primary-source guidance. A document with no lint findings may still be unclear or inaccurate, and a good document may legitimately trigger many findings.

## Review priorities

Fidelity is a prerequisite, not a trade-off against polish. Check the revised text against the source before evaluating style. Preserve facts, numbers, names, attribution, agency, chronology, causal claims, scope, exceptions, negation, modality, and genre. A fluent rewrite that changes any of these is not acceptable.

For each applicable dimension, record **adequate**, **needs revision**, or **not assessable**, with a passage and a reason. Address substantive problems; do not invent defects to fill every category.

## Six dimensions

### 1. Idiomatic wording and voice

Does the wording fit the reader, relationship, and genre? Are phrases awkward in context, or merely familiar? Does repetition obscure the point?

Useful checks:

- An unnecessary announcement such as 「ここでは詳しく見ていきましょう」 may delay the answer.
- 「理由は3つあります」 can help navigation when there really are three reasons; its presence is not a defect by itself.
- Passive voice, dashes, noun endings, repeated polite endings, and contrast structures are legitimate tools. Change them only for a contextual benefit.
- Remove internal agent instructions from reader-facing prose.
- Preserve the author's supported perspective; do not add opinions or experiences to simulate individuality.

### 2. Economy without information loss

Does each passage serve the reader? Delete genuinely redundant introductions, restatements, and decorative emphasis. Keep definitions, examples, safety warnings, exceptions, and accessibility support that carry information or aid comprehension.

Brevity is not the goal by itself. Splitting a sentence, clarifying its actor, or expanding a list may increase length and still improve the document. Do not remove a qualifier to make a sentence more decisive.

### 3. Findability and task usefulness

Can readers locate the answer, procedure, decision, or evidence they need? Are headings informative for this use? Do lists and tables make parallel information easier to compare?

Label headings can be effective in stable reference documents. There is no fixed quota of conclusion headings, bold phrases, bullet points, or sections. Use the format that supports the actual task.

### 4. Logical clarity and evidence

Can readers distinguish observation, inference, opinion, and recommendation? Do reasons support conclusions? Are sources, populations, time periods, units, and comparison bases clear where needed?

Do not infer causation from sequence, convert “some” into “most,” derive attrition from a retention figure without knowing the categories, or add a recommendation unsupported by the source. A descriptive report may properly stop at findings.

### 5. Tone, agency, and honesty

Does the level of courtesy fit the relationship? Are requests still requests, suggestions still suggestions, and commitments still commitments? Is uncertainty visible without empty evasiveness?

For personal prose, preserve feelings and motives actually supplied by the author. For minutes, specifications, and public notices, neutral language may be the correct tone. There is no requirement to inject warmth, frustration, first-person experience, or a personal “why.”

### 6. Consistency between promises and delivery

Does the document deliver what its title and opening promise? Are stated procedures supported by reliable source material? Do examples match the described behavior? Are important limits visible near the relevant claims?

A claim such as 「入力時間が半減する」 must not appear merely because a stronger headline sounds better. Retain the measured condition, population, or tentative status in the headline if necessary.

## Review procedure

1. Identify the audience, purpose, and genre from the request and material; state assumptions that affect the review.
2. Compare source and draft for fidelity. Correct changed meanings first.
3. Review the applicable dimensions and identify the largest reader-facing problems.
4. Revise only where an improvement can be explained. Resolve factual gaps by checking supplied evidence or asking for genuinely missing information, not by adding plausible details.
5. Read the changed passages in context and compare them with the source again. Stop when substantive issues are addressed, retained wording has a reason, and the text serves the request.

Do not require zero findings or a numeric passing score. Do not run an unbounded improvement loop in search of flawless prose. If a change trades one preference for another without improving understanding or meeting the request, keep the source wording.

## Optional scoring

When explicitly requested, use `diagnose.md` for subjective 0–100 estimates and disclosure. These numbers are editorial judgments for an audience, not probabilities or objective measurements. There are no universal pass marks, score bands that classify authors, or score-based editing loops.
